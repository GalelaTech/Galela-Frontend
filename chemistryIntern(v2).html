03

Extraction Chemistry

Everything downstream depended on this stage: accurate mass and volume measurements, correct solvent selection, sample homogeneity, filtration, labelling and contamination prevention. A poorly prepared sample makes even a correctly operating instrument produce unreliable data. This stage introduces the crucial process of extracting the active ingredient that will be used for testing and for formulation at a later stage.

Ocimum basilicum is an aromatic plant that has been used for centuries in traditional medicine and as a culinary herb. Basil leaves contain phenolic compounds, flavonoids and essential oils associated with antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. Two extraction techniques were compared, each differing in how the solvent meets the plant matrix and how energy is supplied to the process.

Figure 8. Ocimum basilicum — plant material used for extraction.

Reflux Extraction

Plant material is heated in solvent under a condenser, so evaporated solvent returns to the vessel instead of being lost. Heat speeds extraction, but temperature has to be controlled because some compounds are heat-sensitive.

heating → evaporation → condensation → return → continuous extraction

Method

Approximately 25 g of basil powder was transferred to a 500 mL round-bottom flask and rinsed with 10 mL distilled water. A further 240 mL of distilled water was added to make up the solvent volume. The condenser setup was prepared as shown. The system was heated for approximately 24 hours while the temperature was maintained below 100 °C.

Figure 9. Reflux extraction setup.
Figure 10. Result after 24 h of reflux heating.
Figure 12. Final reflux extract stored for microbiology and bioassay testing.

Ultrasonic-Assisted Extraction

Ultrasonic energy disrupts plant structures and improves solvent penetration, increasing compound release. It illustrates that extraction methods differ not just by solvent, but by the mechanism used to enhance mass transfer.

ultrasonic energy → structural disruption → improved mass transfer

Method

Approximately 25 g of basil powder was transferred to a conical flask and rinsed with 10 mL ethanol. A further 240 mL of ethanol was added to make the volume up to 250 mL. The flask was placed in an ultrasonicator. The system was sonicated for approximately 2 hours while the temperature was maintained below 25 °C.

Figure 13. Ultrasonic-assisted extraction setup.
Figure 14. Result after 2 h of sonication.
Figure 16. Final ultrasonic extract stored for microbiology and bioassay testing.

Vacuum Filtration

Reduced pressure pulls the liquid extract through a filtration medium, separating it from solid plant residue. The lesson here: extraction isn't finished when a compound enters the solvent — the extract still has to be processed before it's useful.

A direct visual comparison of the two filtration outcomes shows a clear difference in the recovered extract. The reflux filtration yielded a brown filtrate, while the ultrasonic-assisted filtration yielded a distinctly green filtrate — indicating greater release of chlorophyll and other phytochemicals from the plant matrix through ultrasonic cell-wall disruption.

Reflux extract filtration — brown filtrate.
Ultrasonic extract filtration — green filtrate.

Rotary Evaporation and Distillation

extract → reduced pressure + heat + rotation → solvent removal → concentrated extract

Reduced pressure plus rotation and controlled heating lower the solvent's effective boiling point, concentrating the extract efficiently. Skills built: rig set-up, temperature selection, monitoring solvent removal and preventing sample loss. The crude from ultrasonication was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at another facility, producing the concentrated crude shown below.

Figure 17. Concentrated crude extract after rotary evaporation.

Final verdict of this stage

Ultrasonication was found to be more efficient and effective, showing signs of extracting the majority of phytochemicals — observed from the green colour of the filtration, which showed rupture of the basil cell wall and release of chlorophyll along with other phytochemicals. Reflux relied mostly on osmosis between the solvent environment and the interior of the cell, and was therefore less able to diffuse all phytochemicals through the cell wall; it also took a longer period to complete extraction.

04

Microbial Testing

Extraction naturally led into microbiology: first prepare the chemical sample, then investigate what biological effect it produces against each of the microorganisms listed below.

Crude sample preparation

The crude sample from each extraction was weighed at approximately 250 mg and dissolved in approximately 10 mL DMSO solvent before testing.

Agar well diffusion method — crude antimicrobial activity testing

An agar well diffusion assay was performed to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of crude extracts obtained through infusion and ultrasonication. All microbiological procedures were conducted aseptically inside a laminar flow cabinet, with hands, working surfaces and equipment sterilised throughout to minimise contamination.

Separate microbial suspensions of Malassezia furfur and the others followed the same procedure as detailed for M. furfur: they were prepared using approximately 5 mL of sterile water. Fresh bacterial growth was transferred into the corresponding tubes using sterile inoculating loops and mixed thoroughly. The suspensions were then compared with a McFarland standard to standardise the microbial concentration.

The bacterial suspensions were spread evenly onto Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) + 1% butter plates using a sterile spreader. Wells were created in the agar using sterile equipment. Approximately 150 µL of DMSO was added to the control wells, while 150 µL of the infusion or ultrasonic crude extract was added to the respective treatment wells. The plates were incubated for approximately 18–36 hours, after which they were examined for visible zones of inhibition around the wells.

Final verdict of this stage

Reflux crude extract: The crude extract obtained through reflux extraction showed no observable antimicrobial activity against the microorganisms tested, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Cutibacterium acnes, Malassezia furfur, Candida albicans and Streptococcus mutans. Based on the absence of observable inhibition, the reflux extract was not progressed to the MIC stage.

Ultrasonication crude extract: In contrast, the crude extract produced through ultrasonication demonstrated observable antimicrobial activity against the tested microorganisms. This indicated that ultrasonication was more effective at recovering biologically active compounds from the Ocimum basilicum leaves. Due to the observed antimicrobial activity, the ultrasonication crude extract was selected for further investigation through minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing.

Overall: The antimicrobial screening established ultrasonication as the more suitable extraction approach for subsequent MIC investigation, while the reflux extract was excluded from further antimicrobial testing due to its lack of observable activity.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) testing

Only the crude from ultrasonication was used for determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). MIC testing, run by broth dilution, found the lowest concentration of a test substance that stopped visible microbial growth. Lower concentrations gave microorganisms more room to grow; higher concentrations increased the antimicrobial effect until growth was visibly inhibited.

The 96-well microplate format carried over directly into three biological-activity assays — each following the same underlying pattern: an enzyme or radical reacts with a substrate to give a measurable signal, and an active sample reduces that signal.

  1. A sterile 96-well microtiter plate was labelled according to the microorganism being tested (Candida albicans or Malassezia furfur). Rows A–C were designated for test samples and replicates; rows F–H for sample blanks; rows D and E were left empty to separate test and blank sections.
  2. Using a micropipette, 150 µL of the appropriate sterile broth medium was dispensed into wells A1–A12, B1–B12 and C1–C12. The same volume was dispensed into wells F1–F10, G1–G10 and H1–H10, which served as sample blanks.
  3. A volume of 150 µL of the prepared plant extract was added to the first well of each test row (A1, B1, C1) and to the corresponding blank rows (F1, G1, H1).
  4. Two-fold serial dilutions of the extract were performed by transferring 150 µL from well 1 to well 2, mixing thoroughly and continuing sequentially up to well 10 in each row. After mixing in well 10, 150 µL was discarded to ensure equal volumes in all wells. Wells 11 and 12 were reserved for controls.
  5. The microbial inoculum was prepared separately using the test-tube culture method. Fresh cultures of the test microorganisms were suspended in the appropriate broth medium and standardised prior to inoculation.
  6. A volume of 20 µL of the prepared microbial suspension was added to wells A1–A11, B1–B11 and C1–C11. Well 12 in each row was not inoculated.
  7. The blank wells (F1–F10, G1–G10, H1–H10) received no microbial inoculum. These wells contained only broth medium and serially diluted extract and were used as sample blanks to account for the colour and turbidity of the extract.
  8. Well 11 served as the growth control and contained broth medium and microbial inoculum without the test extract. This control was used to confirm the viability and normal growth of the microorganism.
  9. Well 12 served as the sterility control and contained only sterile broth medium. This control was used to confirm the absence of contamination in the media and assay system.
  10. The microtiter plate was sealed and incubated under appropriate conditions for the test microorganism.
  11. Following incubation, the wells were examined for visible microbial growth. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of the extract that showed no visible growth when compared with the growth control.

Biological Activity Assays

The biological assays were conducted on the crude extracts to determine whether they show activity that could be used as a cosmetic ingredient in later formulations.

DPPH Antioxidant Assay

Antioxidants are compounds that help protect cells and biological materials from oxidative damage caused by free radicals and other reactive species. Free radicals can react with important molecules such as lipids, proteins and DNA, potentially contributing to cellular damage and oxidative stress. Measuring the ability of a substance to neutralise free radicals provides an indication of its potential antioxidant activity.

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay is a commonly used method for evaluating free-radical-scavenging activity. DPPH is a stable radical with an intense purple colour. When an antioxidant donates a hydrogen atom or electron to DPPH, the radical is reduced, resulting in a decrease in the intensity of the purple colour. This change is monitored spectrophotometrically at 517 nm, where a greater decrease in absorbance generally indicates stronger radical-scavenging activity. Ascorbic acid is commonly used as a reference antioxidant.

DPPH radical + antioxidant → reduced DPPH → decrease in absorbance
DPPH radical (violet)reduced DPPH (pale)

Experimental method

A DPPH stock solution of 1000 µM was prepared by dissolving 19.72 mg of DPPH in 50 mL, then diluted ten-fold to obtain a 100 µM working solution. The extract stock solution was prepared at 10 mg/mL by dissolving 100 mg of extract in 10 mL. Serial dilutions were prepared at 200, 100, 50, 25 and 10 µg/mL. Ascorbic acid was used as the standard antioxidant at 1 mg/mL, followed by serial dilutions of 100, 50, 25, 10 and 5 µg/mL.

For the assay, 150 µL of the 100 µM DPPH working solution was mixed with 50 µL of the respective extract dilution in the experimental wells. The control consisted of 150 µL DPPH + 50 µL ethanol, while the sample blank contained 150 µL ethanol + 50 µL extract without DPPH. The positive standard consisted of 150 µL DPPH + 50 µL ascorbic acid. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55 and 60 min, with samples shaken before each measurement.

Anti-Tyrosinase Assay

Tyrosinase is a copper-containing enzyme that plays an important role in melanin production, particularly by catalysing the oxidation of L-tyrosine and L-DOPA during melanogenesis. Excessive tyrosinase activity can contribute to increased melanin formation and skin hyperpigmentation. Inhibiting tyrosinase is therefore of interest in cosmetic and pharmaceutical products aimed at controlling melanin production.

The L-DOPA-based tyrosinase inhibition assay determines whether an extract can reduce tyrosinase activity. In the assay, tyrosinase converts L-DOPA into coloured oxidation products, with the resulting change in absorbance monitored spectrophotometrically. If an extract contains compounds capable of inhibiting tyrosinase, less coloured product is formed. Kojic acid is commonly used as a positive control.

tyrosinase + L-DOPA → coloured product

Experimental method

Anti-tyrosinase activity was evaluated in 96-well microplates using mushroom tyrosinase as the enzyme and L-DOPA as the substrate, with kojic acid used as the positive control. Mushroom tyrosinase (100 U/mL) was prepared in PBS (pH 6.8) by dissolving 1 mg of enzyme (1000 U) in 10 mL PBS. L-DOPA (10 mM) was prepared by dissolving 19.72 mg in 10 mL PBS. A 2 mM L-tyrosine solution was prepared by dissolving 3.62 mg in 10 mL PBS.

For the assay, 120 µL PBS, 20 µL tyrosinase and 20 µL of the respective extract were added to each test well and allowed to pre-incubate for 10 min. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 40 µL of 10 mM L-DOPA, giving a final reaction volume of 200 µL. Kojic acid was tested under the same conditions. The plate was incubated at 35 °C, and absorbance was measured at 475 nm at 5-min intervals for 30 min.

The negative control contained 120 µL PBS, 20 µL tyrosinase, 20 µL solvent (ethanol) and 40 µL L-DOPA, while the solvent-free control used water instead of ethanol. A reagent blank containing 160 µL PBS and 40 µL L-DOPA, without enzyme or extract, accounted for the absorbance of the substrate. Sample blanks were prepared by combining 160 µL PBS, 20 µL extract and 20 µL L-DOPA, without tyrosinase, to correct for the intrinsic absorbance of the extracts.

Figure 23. Tyrosinase assay results. The percentage inhibition formula is the same as the one used in the antioxidant assay.

Anti-Elastase Assay

Elastase is a proteolytic enzyme that hydrolyses elastin and other elastin-related proteins, contributing to the breakdown of structural components that help maintain skin firmness and elasticity. Excessive elastase activity has been associated with degradation of the extracellular matrix and processes related to skin ageing. In cosmetic and skincare research, inhibition of elastase is investigated as a potential approach for preserving skin structure and reducing the effects associated with loss of elasticity. In this assay, elastase activity was evaluated using a synthetic substrate that releases a coloured product upon enzymatic hydrolysis.

enzyme + substrate + inhibitor → reduced reaction

Experimental method

Anti-elastase activity was determined using a colorimetric assay based on the hydrolysis of N-succinyl-Ala-Ala-Ala-p-nitroanilide (SANA) by porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE). The release of p-nitroaniline was monitored spectrophotometrically at 410 nm, where a reduction in absorbance indicated inhibition of elastase activity.

A 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) was prepared by dissolving 1.2114 g Tris in approximately 80 mL distilled water, adjusting pH to 8.0 with HCl and making the volume up to 100 mL. PPE was prepared as a 10 U/mL working solution by dissolving 6.25 mg enzyme in 2.5 mL Tris-HCl buffer. The SANA substrate was prepared at 10 mM by dissolving 9.81 mg SANA in 2.5 mL Tris-HCl buffer, with a small volume of DMSO used to facilitate dissolution.

The test extracts were prepared at 20,000 µg/mL by dissolving 200 mg of extract in 10 mL ethanol. Serial two-fold dilutions were prepared to obtain concentrations of 10,000, 5,000, 2,500, 1,250, 625, 312.5, 156.25 and 78.125 µg/mL. Ursolic acid was used as the positive control at an initial 10,000 µg/mL by dissolving 100 mg in 10 mL DMSO, followed by dilution to the required concentrations.

The assay was performed in a 96-well microplate using a final reaction volume of 200 µL per well. Each reaction well contained 140 µL Tris-HCl buffer, 20 µL of the respective extract or ursolic acid solution, and 20 µL of PPE. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to allow interaction between enzyme and test samples. The reaction was initiated by addition of 20 µL of 10 mM SANA substrate, after which the plate was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 410 nm at 5-min intervals.

Solvent controls were included to account for any effects of the extraction solvents on enzyme activity. The ethanol control contained 160 µL buffer, 20 µL PPE, 20 µL ethanol and 20 µL SANA, while the DMSO control contained 160 µL buffer, 20 µL PPE, 20 µL DMSO and 20 µL SANA. A reagent blank containing 180 µL buffer and 20 µL SANA was used to account for background absorbance. Sample blanks containing 180 µL buffer and 20 µL test sample, without enzyme, were included to correct for the intrinsic absorbance of the extracts.

An instrument reading is never automatically the final result. Sample, solvent, reagent and plate background all contribute to the signal — so every value moved through correction and calculation before it became a graph, and only then an interpretation. Some biological work images were not taken due to the hygiene requirements of these sensitive experiments.

Materials Characterization

Here the guiding question changed. In extraction and microbiology it was what compounds can be extracted, and what biological activity do they produce? In characterization it became what is the structure, composition, morphology and thermal behaviour of this material — and did the intended modification actually happen?

Characterization was mainly used to understand active ingredients synthesised elsewhere which would later be incorporated into formulations — such as the Probiotic Lipid Hybrid (PLH) — and to understand modification effects based on measured property changes, as in the EFD clay intercalation study.

Reflection: A Chain of Evidence

The biggest shift over the internship was moving from how to perform an experiment to why it's being performed and what the result means. Rotary evaporation stopped being just a technique once it came with the questions: why concentrate this extract, what will it be used for, how might concentration affect the assay? FTIR and XRD raised the same kind of question — what should the spectrum or pattern show, and does it actually support a real modification?

Biological chain

  1. Plant material
  2. Extraction
  3. Concentrated extract
  4. MIC / antioxidant / enzyme assays
  5. Absorbance measurements
  6. Data processing
  7. Biological interpretation

Materials & formulation chain

  1. Material / extract
  2. Modification / characterization
  3. XRD / FTIR / TEM / SEM / thermal analysis
  4. Structural & chemical confirmation
  5. Formulation development
  6. Stability & functionality testing
  7. Practical application

The degree fed directly into this work: analytical chemistry in standards, dilutions and spectral interpretation; organic chemistry in extraction, solvents and functional groups; physical chemistry in diffusion, solubility, spectroscopy and thermodynamics; inorganic and materials chemistry in the clay and intercalation work. In practice, these branches stopped being separate subjects and started overlapping to solve one problem at a time.

Current capability profile

Chemistry: plant extraction, solvent handling, concentration, functional-group interpretation and sample preparation.

Microbiology: aseptic workflow, microbial suspension preparation, agar diffusion, broth dilution and MIC analysis.

Biological assays: DPPH antioxidant, anti-tyrosinase and anti-elastase workflows with appropriate controls and spectrophotometric measurement.

Materials: practical exposure to FTIR, XRD, TEM, SEM, TGA/TG and DSC and interpretation of complementary evidence.

Cosmetic technology: phase organisation, emulsification, active incorporation, pH adjustment, preservation considerations and application-focused evaluation.

Laboratory research isn't a set of individual experiments — each stage produces evidence that feeds the next, until enough of it points the same way to support a real conclusion.
